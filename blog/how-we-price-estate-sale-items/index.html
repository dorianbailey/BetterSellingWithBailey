Pricing

How We Price Items at Better Selling With Bailey

How I research and price everything from everyday household goods to rare collectibles.

← All posts
Published July 29, 2026About 13 minutes to read

One of the first questions almost every client asks us is some version of the same thing: "How do you know what everything is worth?"

It's a fair question, and it usually comes from a place of genuine concern. People are handing over a house full of belongings, sometimes decades of a parent's or grandparent's life, and trusting a stranger to decide what a price tag should say. That's not a small thing.

Pricing is one of the most important parts of a successful estate sale, and it's also the part that's easiest to get wrong. Price something too high and it sits untouched all weekend. Price it too low and you've quietly cost the client money they didn't know they were losing. Neither mistake is obvious from the outside, which is exactly why it matters so much.

Our goal was never simply to "sell everything." Plenty of items would move fast if we priced them at a dollar. The actual goal is to maximize total proceeds for the family we're working with, which means every price tag has to reflect real research, not a guess.

Why Proper Pricing Matters

Pricing is the foundation everything else in an estate sale is built on. Good staging and marketing bring people through the door, but pricing determines whether those people actually buy — and how much money ends up in the client's pocket at the end of the weekend.

When pricing is off, it rarely fails loudly. A piece of furniture priced too high doesn't get an angry reaction; it just gets passed over. A collectible priced too low sells in the first ten minutes, and nobody ever finds out it could have brought three times as much. Incorrect pricing quietly erodes profit in both directions, and most of that erosion is invisible unless someone is watching closely.

Emotional attachment complicates this further. It's natural for a family to remember what something cost originally, or to feel that a piece tied to a parent or grandparent should be worth more simply because of what it meant to them. We understand that completely, but the market doesn't price sentiment. Our job is to separate what an item means emotionally from what it's actually worth today, while still treating the item — and the family's feelings about it — with respect.

There's also a balance to strike between fair market value and the actual price something will realistically sell for during a two- or three-day sale. A museum might wait years for the right buyer at the right price. We don't have that luxury, so part of pricing correctly is understanding not just what an item is worth, but what it's worth this weekend, to the buyers who are actually going to walk through the door.

Every Estate Is Different

People sometimes ask if we have a master price list or a spreadsheet of standard values we apply to every sale. We don't, and honestly, anyone who tells you they do is skipping a step. There is no universal pricing sheet that works across estates, because no two collections are alike.

Location matters more than most people expect. A set of tools that would sell quickly in one part of Erie County might sit for weeks in another, simply because of who lives nearby and what they're looking for. Brand matters too — two end tables that look nearly identical can have completely different values if one carries a name collectors recognize and the other doesn't. Condition changes everything, sometimes by a factor of ten. And demand is never static; what was popular two years ago isn't necessarily popular now.

This is why two items that look identical sitting side by side can have dramatically different price tags. A stranger walking through a sale might see two similar-looking lamps and assume we priced one wrong. In reality, one might be a mass-produced piece from a big box store and the other a discontinued design from a manufacturer with an active collector base. Nothing about their appearance tells you that. The research does.

Our Research Process

Every item we price goes through some version of the same process, though the depth of that process depends on what we're looking at. For everyday household goods, it might take seconds, drawing on experience from hundreds of past sales. For a rare or unusual item, it can take considerably longer.

We start by looking at recently sold listings rather than asking prices, and the distinction matters enormously — more on that in a moment. We compare data across multiple marketplaces rather than relying on a single source, because any one platform can have its own quirks and biases. For collectibles, we dig into manufacturer information, production dates, and whether a piece was part of a limited run or a discontinued line.

We also look closely at rarity versus how common something actually is. An item can feel rare to someone who's never seen one before while actually being one of thousands still in circulation. Conversely, something ordinary-looking might be scarce in the specific condition or configuration it's in. We check condition carefully, since even small differences — a chip, a missing piece, a faded label — can move a price significantly.

Sold prices are far more valuable than asking prices, and this is one of the most important distinctions in the entire process. Anyone can list an item for whatever number they want. What matters is what buyers actually paid, in similar condition, in a comparable time frame. We also never let a single online listing determine a price on its own. One listing is a data point. A pattern across many sales is a market.

What Factors Affect Value?

A long list of variables feeds into every price we set, and most of them interact with each other rather than working in isolation.

  • Brand — Recognized names in furniture, tools, kitchenware, and collectibles almost always command a premium over unbranded or generic equivalents.
  • Age — Age alone doesn't create value, but it can contribute to it when paired with scarcity or historical interest.
  • Condition — Wear, damage, repairs, and restoration all affect what a buyer is willing to pay, sometimes dramatically.
  • Completeness — A board game missing pieces, a tool set missing attachments, or a china set missing serving pieces is worth a fraction of a complete set.
  • Original packaging — For toys, electronics, and collectibles especially, original boxes and inserts can significantly increase value.
  • Color variations — Certain colorways of the same item, from cookware to sneakers to glassware, are more sought-after than others.
  • Limited editions — A production run that ended after a limited number of units almost always sells for more than an ongoing, unlimited line.
  • Local demand — What sells well in Erie County isn't always what sells well nationally, and we price with our actual local buyers in mind.
  • National demand — Some categories, particularly collectibles, are driven by buyers well outside our region.
  • Seasonal demand — Holiday décor, outdoor equipment, and seasonal clothing shift in value depending on the time of year we're holding the sale.
  • Current market trends — Collector interest moves over time, and pricing has to reflect where the market is now, not where it was five years ago.

As an example, we'll sometimes see two nearly identical sets of vintage glassware where one is priced noticeably higher because it comes in a color variation collectors specifically look for. Nothing about the shape or maker's mark is different — the color alone changes the value.

Not Everything Is Worth What the Internet Says

This might be the single biggest source of misunderstanding in the entire pricing conversation, and we address it with clients constantly. Someone will find a similar item listed online for an eye-popping number and reasonably assume theirs is worth the same. Unfortunately, an asking price and a sale price are two very different things.

eBay in particular is full of unrealistic asking prices. Anyone can list an item for whatever they'd like, and plenty of sellers list high and simply wait, sometimes for months or years, without ever finding a buyer at that number. An active, unsold listing tells you almost nothing about real market value. What matters is what similar items have actually sold for, and how consistently.

Viral misinformation is another factor. Stories about a rare find selling for an enormous sum tend to spread quickly, and people naturally wonder if they own something similar. Occasionally they do. Far more often, the item that sold for a remarkable price had specific characteristics — an error, a rare variant, exceptional condition, documented provenance — that don't apply to a similar-looking piece.

Auction results can be useful, but a single unusual auction outcome doesn't establish a market on its own, especially if two determined bidders drove the price up in the moment. Facebook Marketplace pricing tends to run even less reliable, since listings there are rarely vetted or based on research at all. Professional pricing means looking at the pattern across many data points rather than anchoring to one outlier, no matter how compelling that outlier is.

How We Price Everyday Household Items

The bulk of any estate is everyday household goods — kitchenware, furniture, tools, garage items, holiday decorations, books, clothing, glassware, home décor, and small appliances. These items are priced differently than collectibles, and generally more quickly, because we're drawing on consistent patterns from hundreds of past sales rather than researching each piece from scratch.

For these categories, we're weighing current used-market value, condition, brand recognition, and how quickly similar items typically move at estate sales in this area. A good working kitchen mixer from a known brand prices differently than an off-brand version, even if both function the same way. A solid wood dresser prices differently than particleboard furniture of a similar style, and buyers in our area generally know the difference.

Tools and garage items tend to sell briskly when priced realistically, since there's steady local demand for functional equipment. Holiday decorations swing with the calendar. Books, unless they're notable first editions or signed copies, are priced to move rather than priced as collectibles, since the used book market is saturated almost everywhere. The goal with everyday goods is efficient, fair pricing that reflects real resale value and keeps the sale moving.

How We Price Collectibles

Collectibles are a different conversation entirely, and they're where our research process gets the most intensive. This category includes sports cards, Pokémon cards, LEGO sets, vintage toys, coins, advertising memorabilia, fishing equipment, hunting gear, firearms (pricing only — we don't offer legal advice on firearm transfers), military items, vintage electronics, records, comics, and video games.

Each of these categories has its own internal logic. Sports cards and Pokémon cards are driven by condition grading, print rarity, and player or character popularity, and values can shift week to week based on performance or cultural relevance. LEGO sets depend heavily on whether the set is retired, complete, and whether the original box and instructions are present. Vintage toys hinge on maker, condition, and whether they still have original packaging.

Coins require checking mint marks, year, condition, and metal content, since a coin's face value and its collector value are often worlds apart. Firearms pricing depends on manufacturer, model, condition, and documented history, and we handle that research carefully and separately from any legal transfer requirements, which we leave to licensed dealers. Records, comics, and vintage electronics all depend on specific pressing, edition, or model details that aren't visible at a glance.

Collectibles require significantly more research than household goods because the value swings are so much larger. Being off by a little on a kitchen item might mean a few dollars either way. Being off on a rare collectible can mean a difference of hundreds of dollars, which is exactly why we slow down and dig deeper on anything that falls into this category.

Do We Ever Bring in Specialists?

Most estates, even ones with genuinely valuable pieces, can be priced accurately through the research process described above, combined with experience from the estates we've already handled. That covers the overwhelming majority of what we encounter.

Occasionally, though, we come across something exceptionally rare or highly specialized — a piece that falls outside what general research can confidently confirm. In those cases, we'll consult a subject-matter expert rather than guess. It doesn't happen often, but when it does, we think it's a better outcome for the client than pricing something significant based on incomplete information.

Should Everything Be Expensive?

No, and this surprises some clients at first. It might seem like pricing everything as high as possible would maximize proceeds, but that's not how estate sales actually work in practice.

Fast-moving inventory creates momentum. Buyers who see a sale full of fair, realistic prices tend to buy more, and buy more confidently, than buyers who sense they're being tested with inflated numbers. Buyer psychology plays a real role here — perceived fairness makes people comfortable spending, and that comfort translates into more items leaving the house rather than sitting untouched.

There's also the reality of clearing an entire estate, not just selling the standout pieces. A sale full of fairly priced everyday items alongside a handful of correctly priced valuable pieces tends to outperform a sale where everything is priced aggressively and half of it goes home unsold. Our goal is the best total outcome for the whole estate, not the highest price tag on any single item.

Can Clients Help With Pricing?

Absolutely, and we welcome it. Clients often know details we can't get any other way — where a piece came from, whether it was a gift, whether it was restored or repaired at some point, or how it was used over the years. That history is genuinely useful and we always want to hear it.

What we can't do is base the final price on sentimental value or on what something originally cost decades ago. Current market value is determined through research and experience, not the emotional or historical weight an item carries for the family. We see this as a collaboration: the client brings the story, and we bring the research, and the two combine into a price that's both informed and fair.

Common Estate Sale Pricing Myths

A few misconceptions come up constantly, and it's worth addressing them directly.

  • "Older means valuable." Age alone rarely creates value. Plenty of genuinely old items are common and worth very little, while some newer items are worth considerably more due to demand or scarcity.
  • "Heavy furniture is worth a fortune." Weight is not a value indicator. Plenty of heavy furniture is simply heavy, not valuable, while some lightweight pieces are worth far more due to maker or design.
  • "Everything antique is expensive." The antique market has cooled significantly for many categories over the past two decades. Plenty of genuine antiques sell for modest prices today.
  • "If it's listed online for $2,000, it's worth $2,000." As covered above, an asking price is not a sale price, and one listing doesn't establish a market.
  • "Original purchase price determines current value." What something cost when new rarely reflects what it's worth on the used and collector markets today, in either direction.

Our Goal Is Simple

Our goal is to maximize total proceeds for the client — not to inflate prices, and not to rush inventory out the door for the sake of a clean sale. Those two things can pull against each other, and getting the balance right is the entire point of our research process.

That means real transparency about how we arrived at a price, genuine research behind every number on every tag, and treating every client's belongings — valuable or not, sentimental or not — with the same level of care and respect.

Pricing is one of the biggest advantages of hiring a professional estate sale company rather than handling a sale independently. It's also one of the hardest parts to get right without experience, since the mistakes are quiet and easy to miss until the sale is already over.

Pricing questions

How do estate sale companies determine prices?

We research recently sold prices (not asking prices) across multiple marketplaces, then factor in brand, age, condition, completeness, and current demand. Everyday household goods draw on experience from past sales, while collectibles get deeper individual research into rarity, manufacturer details, and market trends before a price is set.

Do estate sale companies use eBay?

Yes, eBay is one of several resources we check, but we focus specifically on completed and sold listings rather than active asking prices. Anyone can list an item for any price on eBay, so an unsold listing tells us very little on its own. We look for consistent patterns across sold items rather than relying on a single listing.

Are estate sale prices negotiable?

In most cases, yes, especially as a sale progresses or on the final day. Prices typically start firm and become more flexible as we move through the sale, which is standard practice across the industry and helps clear remaining inventory efficiently.

What if I think something is worth more?

We're always happy to discuss specific items with clients before the sale. If you have information about an item's history, documentation, or provenance that could affect its value, share it with us. We'll factor that into our research, though the final price still needs to reflect what the current market will realistically support.

Should I get an appraisal before an estate sale?

For most household items and typical collectibles, our research process is sufficient and a separate appraisal isn't necessary. For exceptionally rare or highly specialized items, we may recommend consulting a specialist ourselves, so you generally don't need to arrange this on your own beforehand.

How long does pricing an estate take?

It depends heavily on the size of the estate and how many collectible or specialty items are involved. A typical home might take a few days of research and preparation, while estates with significant collections can take longer since each piece in those categories gets individual attention.

What happens if an item doesn't sell?

We discuss options with the client before the sale ends, which can include donation, consignment, or removal. Part of our service includes handling leftover items so the property is left clean and ready, without the client needing to manage what remains on their own.

Can I remove certain items before the sale?

Absolutely. Any items you'd like to keep, gift to family, or handle separately can be set aside before we begin pricing and staging. Just let us know in advance so we can plan the sale around what's actually available to sell.
