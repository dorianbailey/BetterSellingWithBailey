An executor's guide to the house contents in Pennsylvania

How the contents are valued, the dates to know, and what to do before anything is sold.

← All posts
By Dorian BaileyPublished November 14, 2026About 4 minutes to read

Short answer: as executor in Pennsylvania, the house contents are part of the estate. You list them at their fair market value on the date of death, on the estate inventory and the inheritance tax return. The will decides who gets what, and an estate sale can turn the rest into money for the estate, with a written record of every sale.

What counts as the house contents

Furniture, antiques, jewelry, vehicles, tools, collectibles and everyday household goods. Pennsylvania's Department of Revenue treats all real and tangible personal property of a Pennsylvania resident, including furniture, antiques and jewelry, as subject to inheritance tax.

How the contents are valued

Value is generally based on fair market value as of the date of death: what the items would actually sell for, not what was paid for them. Everyday household goods are usually worth far less than families expect. A few things, like jewelry, art or a serious collection, can be worth much more. Ask the estate attorney whether any of them need a formal appraisal.

A free estate sale walkthrough gives you a realistic idea of what the contents could bring, which helps when you sit down with the attorney or accountant.

The dates to know

  • Inheritance tax: the return is due within nine months of the death, and paying within three months earns a 5% discount.
  • Estate inventory: the executor files a verified inventory with the Register of Wills, no later than the first accounting or the inheritance tax return's due date, whichever comes first. An interested party can ask for it sooner.
  • Things found later: if more property turns up after the inventory is filed, a supplemental inventory follows.

Your attorney will tell you exactly which dates apply to your estate. My first 30 days checklist puts the house and the paperwork in order.

Before anything is sold

  1. Get your authority. In Erie County, the Register of Wills issues letters testamentary when there's a will, or letters of administration when there isn't.
  2. Check the will for specific gifts, and set those items aside.
  3. Let the family choose keepsakes, and write down who takes what.
  4. Ask the attorney what can be sold before the estate is settled.
  5. Don't throw anything away yet. Here's what to hold on to.

How an estate sale helps an executor

  • A record for the estate's books. You're paid the day the sale ends, with a written statement of what sold and what was deducted. Here's what the statement shows.
  • One clear cost. A flat $995 plus 25% of sales, taken from the proceeds. How that compares in Erie.
  • An empty house by your date. Leftovers are kept, donated or removed free, so the house can be listed or handed over.
  • You don't have to be there. Useful when the executor lives out of town.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. An estate attorney can tell you exactly what applies to your family's estate.

Common questions

Do household contents count for Pennsylvania inheritance tax?

Yes. Pennsylvania taxes the tangible personal property of a resident who has died, including furniture, antiques and jewelry, at its fair market value on the date of death.

When is the Pennsylvania inheritance tax return due?

Within nine months of the death. Paying within three months earns a 5% discount. Your estate attorney or accountant can confirm the details for your estate.

Can an executor hold an estate sale before the estate is settled?

Often, but ask the estate attorney first. Set aside anything the will gives to a specific person, let the family choose keepsakes, and keep a written record of what was sold.
